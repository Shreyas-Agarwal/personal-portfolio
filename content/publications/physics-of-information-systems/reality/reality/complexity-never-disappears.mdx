# Complexity Never Disappears

A washing machine has, on most models, one dial and a handful of buttons. You choose a fabric
setting, close the door, press start, and walk away. It is, by design, one of the simplest
interfaces in an ordinary home — simpler than a thermostat, simpler than a car dashboard, simpler by
a wide margin than the device most people use to select the wash cycle in the first place.

None of that simplicity means the underlying problem got simpler. Behind that one dial sits a
solenoid valve system regulating water intake, a heating element with its own thermal safety
cutoffs, a drum motor with variable-speed control tuned separately for wash, rinse, and spin, a
drainage pump with its own failure modes, a suspension system damping the drum's movement so a spin
cycle doesn't walk the machine across the floor, and — on most machines built in the last two
decades — a small embedded computer arbitrating all of it against sensors for water level, load
balance, and door-lock status. The complexity of "get clothes clean without flooding the kitchen or
destroying the fabric" did not go away when the interface shrank to one dial. It went *somewhere*.
It went into the engineering of everything behind the panel, so that the person standing in front of
the panel would never have to think about any of it.

This is worth naming as a law, because it behaves like one: **the total complexity of a system does
not decrease when you simplify an interface to it. It is redistributed — moved from the part of the
system a person interacts with to some other part of the system that now has to absorb it instead.**
The HCI researcher Larry Tesler called an early version of this idea the Law of Conservation of
Complexity: every application has a certain amount of irreducible complexity, and the only real
design choice is *who* deals with it — the user, or the engineer who built the thing the user is
using.

<Callout type="architectural-tension">
Simplicity, wherever it appears, is not the absence of complexity. It is a claim that someone, somewhere — a person, a team, a piece of hardware — has already agreed to hold the complexity that was removed from view.
</Callout>

## The factory that specialized its way to a new problem

In 1913, Henry Ford's Highland Park plant introduced the moving assembly line for automobile
production, cutting the time to build a Model T chassis from roughly twelve hours to about ninety
minutes. The popular version of this story treats it as a simplification: building a car got
*easier*. It didn't. Building a car remained exactly as complex as it had been the day before — the
same thousands of parts, the same tolerances, the same physics of an internal combustion engine.
What changed is that the complexity, previously held inside the head of a single generalist mechanic
who assembled a whole car more or less from start to finish, got broken apart and redistributed
across dozens of specialized stations, each one now individually simpler than the old mechanic's
job, but collectively requiring something the old model never needed at all: a rigidly timed,
tightly coordinated line, where a delay at any one station now stalled every station behind it.

The complexity of building a car did not shrink. It moved — out of the individual worker's head, and
into the coordination problem of the line itself. Ford's plant didn't eliminate complexity. It
relocated it to a place where it could be engineered deliberately instead of being carried,
invisibly and unevenly, inside the judgment of whichever mechanic happened to be assigned that
chassis.

## When the hidden complexity comes due

The most sobering demonstration of this principle at scale comes from commercial aviation, in a
well-documented pattern researchers in flight safety call the *automation paradox*: the more capable
and reliable an aircraft's automated systems become at handling routine flight, the less practiced
its human pilots remain at handling the flight manually — which matters enormously in the rare
moments when the automation itself fails or hands control back unexpectedly, often with very little
warning, in exactly the situations that are hardest to fly by hand.

<Callout type="unresolved">
The Air France 447 accident in 2009 is often cited in aviation safety literature as a case study of this pattern — specific details of that flight should be verified against official investigation reports before being cited publicly, as with any named historical incident.
</Callout>

The broader pattern, independent of any one incident, is what matters for this chapter: automating a
task doesn't remove the complexity of that task from the system. It moves the complexity out of
routine, everyday practice — where skill would ordinarily be maintained through repetition — and
into the design of training programs, simulator drills, and emergency procedures meant to keep a
skill sharp that is, by design, almost never used. When that design is done well, the relocated
complexity is held safely, out of sight, exactly the way the washing machine's plumbing holds its
complexity safely behind one dial. When it isn't held well, the complexity doesn't vanish just
because nobody built anywhere for it to live. It waits, and it tends to surface at the single worst
possible moment — under time pressure, with degraded information, when whoever is holding it is
least prepared to receive it.

## A conservation law, not a design flaw

It's worth being precise about what kind of claim this is. It is not an argument that simplification
is bad, or that interfaces shouldn't be simplified, or that automation is a mistake. The washing
machine's one dial is a genuine achievement; nobody is worse off for not needing to understand
solenoid valves to do laundry. The claim is narrower and, in a way, more useful than a warning
against simplification: **whenever you see complexity disappear from one place, look for where it
went, because it did not stop existing.** It is sitting somewhere else in the system now — in an
engineering team, in a training program, in an on-call rotation, in a support contract, in a piece
of documentation nobody reads until the day they desperately need to — and the quality of a system
has less to do with how little complexity it appears to have, and much more to do with whether the
place that complexity was relocated to is actually equipped to hold it.

This reframes a question that comes up constantly in the design of any information system, technical
or organizational: "can we make this simpler?" The honest version of that question is never really
about deletion. It's about relocation. *Simpler for whom, and at whose expense?* A dashboard that
hides operational detail behind a single health score has not made the operation simpler — it has
moved the complexity of interpreting operational health away from the dashboard's viewer and onto
whoever built the scoring logic behind it, who now silently carries the responsibility of getting
that compression right on the viewer's behalf, indefinitely, without the viewer ever being reminded
that a compression is happening at all.

## Closing the first monograph

Three chapters, three versions of the same underlying fact. Reality happens, and only afterward does
information about it exist — nothing can be known about the world except after a delay, however
small. Every representation of that reality is a chosen compression, shaped by the decision it was
built to serve, never a neutral or complete copy — a map is not a smaller reality, it's a reality
with something specific left out on purpose. And whatever gets left out does not stop existing just
because it's no longer visible — it moves, and it comes due somewhere else, to someone else, usually
without warning.

These three claims are really one claim, seen from three angles: representation always costs something, and that cost cannot be waived, only assigned. Every system this series will look at from
here — every cache, every model, every dashboard, every distributed consensus protocol, every
organization trying to coordinate people who can't all see the same reality at the same time — is
downstream of this. Reality exists before information. The territory exists before the map. And
whatever the map leaves out is still out there, still real, still someone's problem, just not yet
visible from where you're currently standing.
