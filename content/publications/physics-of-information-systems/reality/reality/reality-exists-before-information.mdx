# Reality Exists Before Information

Say the word "fire" out loud, in an empty room. Nothing burns. Nothing warms. Nothing lights up the
dark.[^1] This is not surprising to anyone, which is precisely why it's worth pausing on: the word
for the thing and the thing itself are so obviously different in kind that we never confuse them,
not even for a second, not even as children. A three-year-old who has just learned the word "hot"
does not expect the syllable itself to burn her hand. She understood, before she could have
explained it, that a sound standing for a phenomenon is not the phenomenon. It is something else — a
token, a stand-in, a sign — pointing at the thing without ever becoming it.

This chapter is about that gap, and it is important to be precise about which gap, because there is
a second one, close enough to be mistaken for it, that belongs to a different conversation entirely.
It would be easy to read everything that follows as being about *delay* — about information lagging
behind the events it describes, arriving late, going stale. That is a real and important problem,
and this series will spend real time on it. But it is not this chapter's problem. This chapter's
claim is stranger and, if anything, more fundamental: **even if a representation could somehow
arrive with zero delay — instantly, the very moment the reality it describes came into being — it
would still not be that reality. It would still only be a sign of it.** The gap here isn't a matter
of timing. It would remain exactly as wide if time were removed from the problem altogether.

## The banknote and the vault

For most of the history of paper currency, a banknote was, quite literally, a receipt. Early banks
issuing paper money were making a specific, checkable promise: bring this note back to us, and we
will hand you the corresponding weight of gold or silver we are holding in a vault on your behalf.
The note itself was worth nothing as an object — a small rectangle of cotton-linen fiber and ink.
Its entire value came from what it *stood for*: a claim on something else, held somewhere else, made
of something else.

What matters for this chapter isn't that the promise was sometimes broken, or that the gold might
have been slow to fetch, or that a bank might have been a day's ride away. Set all of that aside.
Imagine the least delayed version of this system possible: the vault is next door, the teller is
instant, the exchange rate is perfectly accurate and updates in real time. Even in that frictionless
version, the note in your hand is still not the gold. It is a *symbol* of a claim on the gold. You
cannot melt it down. You cannot use it to fill a tooth or plate a circuit. Its entire usefulness
depends on everyone agreeing to treat the symbol as though it carried the value of the referent,
while never actually being the referent itself. This is not a flaw in how currency works. It is the
entire mechanism by which currency works — value, abstracted into a token light enough to carry in a
pocket, precisely because the token is *not* the thing.

<Callout type="architectural-tension">
A system can be made faster, more accurate, and more current without ever closing the gap between a sign and what it signifies. Speed and fidelity are properties of the relationship between a representation and reality. They are not a path toward the representation becoming reality.
</Callout>

## The score and the sound

A page of sheet music contains no sound whatsoever. It contains ink — dots, lines, curved stems —
arranged according to a notation system that a trained musician can read and internally convert into
an expectation of pitch, rhythm, and dynamics. A silent room with a score in it is exactly as silent
as a room with nothing in it at all. The music the score describes does not exist anywhere on the
page. It exists, if it exists anywhere, in the air, as vibrating pressure waves, produced by a
performer reading the marks and doing something physical — drawing a bow, pressing a key, moving
breath through a reed — that the marks themselves cannot do.

This is worth sitting with because a score is, by any reasonable standard, an extremely *good*
representation. A skilled conductor can read an orchestral score silently and report back, with real
precision, what the piece will sound like — tempo, texture, which instruments enter where. The
representation can be extraordinarily faithful. Faithfulness is not the issue. However faithful the
score becomes, it remains a different *kind* of object from the sound it describes: one is a
physical vibration in air, perceived by an ear; the other is graphite or ink arranged on paper,
perceived by an eye and interpreted by training. No degree of improvement to the notation — more
precise dynamic markings, finer-grained tempo instructions — moves the score even slightly closer to
*being* sound. It only makes it a better set of instructions for producing sound, which is a
different kind of achievement altogether.

## The chart and the lungs

A patient's medical chart lists a diagnosis code — in the standard international classification,
asthma is recorded as J45.909. Somewhere in a database, that alphanumeric string sits in a field,
associated with a patient ID, timestamped, indexed, queryable. The code does not wheeze. It does not
tighten under exertion or ease with a bronchodilator. Whatever is actually happening in a real
patient's real airways — smooth muscle constricting, mucus production increasing, air moving with
real physical difficulty through real physical passages — is happening in tissue, not in a database
field. J45.909 is a pointer to that condition, agreed upon by international medical convention so
that a doctor in Tokyo and a doctor in Toronto can both know, unambiguously, what the other means by
it. It is an extremely useful pointer. It is still, categorically, just a pointer. You could update
that database field a million times a second, with perfect real-time accuracy tracking the patient's
exact condition from moment to moment, and the field would still be a string of characters in a
table, and the patient's lungs would still be a separate, physical thing that the string merely
refers to.

<Callout type="unresolved">
There is no engineering effort — more sensors, faster pipelines, tighter synchronization — that converts a representation into the reality it represents. Reducing the gap in time and reducing the gap in kind are not the same project, and closing the first one entirely would not touch the second one at all.
</Callout>

## Two different gaps, and why the difference matters

It's worth being explicit about why this distinction earns its own chapter rather than folding
neatly into a later one about timing. Later in this series, in the Time monograph, there is a real
and separate argument to be made that reality always arrives late — that between an event happening
and a system registering it, there is an irreducible delay, however small, and that this delay has
serious architectural consequences: for logical clocks, for causal ordering, for the very idea of a
single shared "now" across a distributed system. That is a claim about *when* information exists
relative to the event it describes.

This chapter's claim survives even in a universe where that delay is engineered down to zero.
Suppose, purely as a thought experiment, a sensor existed that reported the exact state of some part
of reality with no lag whatsoever — the instant a leaf falls, a database field updates,
simultaneously, with no propagation delay at all. Even in that impossible, frictionless case, the
database field would still be a symbol — a stored pattern of bits, agreed by convention to
correspond to "a leaf has fallen" — and the leaf would still be an actual leaf, made of cellulose,
now lying on actual ground. Removing the delay removes one gap. It does not touch the other one. The
two gaps are corollaries of each other in ordinary practice — they nearly always show up together,
and a system straining against one will often find itself straining against both — but they are not
the same gap, and conflating them is exactly the mistake this chapter exists to head off before the
rest of the series builds on it.

## What a system actually stores

Put plainly, in language the rest of this monograph and the ones after it will keep returning to:
**an information system never stores reality. It stores signs — symbols, tokens, encodings — that a
community of use has agreed to treat as standing for reality, in roughly the same spirit that a
nation agrees a small rectangle of printed cotton stands for a claim on gold.** This is not a
limitation of current technology, waiting on some future engineering breakthrough to overcome. It is
closer to a fact about what representation *is*. A sign that became fully identical to its referent
would stop being useful as a sign — it would be as unwieldy, as unfoldable, as impossible to carry
around and query and compare against other signs, as the empire-sized map that coincides point for
point with the empire it maps. Usefulness and non-identity are not in tension here. Usefulness
*requires* non-identity. A representation earns its keep precisely by being a different, smaller,
more portable kind of thing than what it represents — and that difference in kind is what this
chapter has been pointing at all along, underneath every fire that doesn't burn, every banknote that
isn't gold, every score that makes no sound, every code that doesn't wheeze.

This is the ground the rest of the Reality monograph is built on. Because a representation is,
categorically, never its referent, there is always a specific, examinable question about what a
given representation chose to carry over and what it left out — chose being the operative word,
because the leaving-out is rarely accidental. That is the question the next chapter takes up
directly.

| The word "fire" | Combustion, heat, light | The syllable does not burn |
| A banknote | Gold held in a vault | The paper cannot fill a tooth |
| A musical score | Vibrating air, heard as sound | Ink on paper makes no noise |
| Diagnosis code `J45.909` | Constricted airways, real tissue | The string does not wheeze |

</Figure>

<FormalNote>
Semiotics gives the chapter's four examples one shared name. A sign, in Ferdinand de Saussure's
formulation, pairs a *signifier* (the note, the score, the code) with a *signified* (the gold, the
sound, the condition) — a pairing fixed by convention, never by resemblance:

$$\text{sign} = \langle \text{signifier}, \text{signified} \rangle, \qquad \text{signifier} \not\equiv \text{signified}$$

No amount of engineering closes that $\not\equiv$. It names what makes something a sign rather than a
duplicate.
</FormalNote>
