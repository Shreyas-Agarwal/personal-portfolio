Formula 1's fuel-flow regulation is, on paper, almost insultingly simple: an engine may not burn more than 100 kilograms of fuel per hour, full stop, enforced by a single homologated sensor sitting in the fuel line between tank and engine, built by an independent supplier so no team could tamper with the measurement itself. For most of the 2019 season, one team's engine was reportedly producing straight-line speed that had the rest of the paddock quietly doing arithmetic that didn't add up, and by late in the year, suspicion had hardened into an FIA technical investigation that, in early 2020, ended not in a public ruling but in a confidential settlement — no findings released, no wrongdoing formally established, the exact mechanism never officially confirmed by either party. What follows, then, is not a proven account. It is the reconstruction that F1 technical journalists pieced together afterward, from the regulations, the sensor's known specifications, and the physics of what would have to be true for the numbers to work — and it deserves to be read as exactly that: an alleged mechanism, not a settled fact, presented here because whether or not it happened, it is a genuinely beautiful piece of engineering theory.

The sensor at the center of it, built to a homologated FIA specification, was understood to take a fresh flow measurement roughly every 0.45 milliseconds — on the order of 2,200 readings every second — but the number that actually mattered for compliance wasn't any single one of those readings. It was a rolling average, computed over a longer window reportedly on the order of tens of milliseconds, smoothing out exactly the kind of brief, sharp variation any real fuel system produces from moment to moment. The theory, as reconstructed by outlets like *Auto Motor und Sport*, was that an engine could be tuned to spike its actual, instantaneous fuel flow dramatically above the 100 kg/h limit for a window measured in single-digit milliseconds — genuinely illegal, in that instant — and then drop it correspondingly below the limit for the rest of the averaging window, so that by the time the rolling average was computed, the number reported back to the regulators sat comfortably, legally, right at the ceiling the whole time. If true, the engineering demanded here is extraordinary: a fuel system precise enough to spike and recover within a window narrower than the blink of an eye, timed with enough consistency, thousands of times per minute, that the sensor's own averaging — the very mechanism meant to smooth out noise and produce a fair, stable reading — became the exact camouflage the spike needed to hide inside.

<Callout type="unresolved">
No official source has ever confirmed the specific sampling rate, averaging window, or spike duration involved in this case — the matter was resolved confidentially, and every number above is a reconstruction by technical journalists, not a disclosed fact. It is included here as the clearest available illustration of a real and well-understood engineering vulnerability, not as a settled account of what actually happened in 2019.
</Callout>

## The wheel that spins backward on film

Whether or not this specific story holds up in every detail, the vulnerability it describes is entirely real, and has a name: aliasing. It's the same phenomenon behind a much older and far less contested example. Watch an old western, and at some point a stagecoach will roll past the camera, its spoked wheel turning fast — and the wheel will appear to slow down, stop entirely, or spin gently backward, even though the coach is clearly moving forward at a steady clip. Nothing is wrong with the wheel. What's "wrong" is the camera: it records the scene not as continuous motion but as a sequence of still frames, captured roughly twenty-four times a second, and if the wheel's spokes complete very close to one full rotation, or two, or three, between one frame and the next, successive frames catch a spoke in nearly the same position each time — and the eye reconstructs that near-stillness as a wheel that has slowed to a crawl or reversed direction. The wheel's true rotation never changed. The camera simply stopped looking often enough to keep up with it, and the gaps between glances got mistaken, by the viewer, for the truth.

This is precisely the structure the alleged fuel-flow mechanism depends on, if it happened at all: a real, fast oscillation in the underlying quantity, occurring at a rate the measuring instrument's sampling window was never built to catch, folding down into a false, steady, legal-looking reading — the fuel-flow equivalent of a wheel that appears to stand still while the coach rolls on.

## The number that tells you how often is enough

This isn't a vague problem with a vague solution. It has an exact mathematical answer, discovered independently by the physicist Harry Nyquist in the 1920s and formalized rigorously by the mathematician and engineer Claude Shannon two decades later, now known as the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem: to correctly reconstruct a signal that changes no faster than some maximum frequency, you must sample it at a rate at least twice that frequency. Sample any slower, and it is not merely that you'll have gaps in your data — you will actively construct a false signal, a lower, invented frequency called an alias, that looks like real information but describes nothing that ever actually happened.

The tool that makes this precise is the Fourier transform, developed by the French mathematician Joseph Fourier in the early 1800s while studying heat, which decomposes any signal — a sound wave, a vibration, a stream of sensor readings — into the sum of simple, pure oscillations at different frequencies. A complex signal that looks like meaningless noise in its raw form, plotted against time, often turns out, once passed through a Fourier transform into what's called the frequency domain, to be made of just a handful of clean, identifiable frequencies layered on top of each other. This is the tool that lets an engineer ask, precisely, "what is the fastest meaningful frequency actually present in this signal" — the exact number the Nyquist rate depends on.

<Callout type="architectural-tension">
Sampling rate is not a technical afterthought. It is a decision about which frequencies of reality a system is even capable of seeing. Anything oscillating faster than half the sampling rate does not simply go unmeasured — it gets measured incorrectly, disguised as something slower and false.
</Callout>

## What spectral analysis would actually look for

If regulators wanted to investigate a claim like this properly, the tool for the job is spectral analysis — passing a signal through a Fourier transform and examining the resulting frequency spectrum for periodicity that shouldn't be there. A fuel flow that spikes and drops at a regular, hidden frequency would show up, in a full-resolution frequency-domain analysis, as a distinct spike at that specific frequency — a fingerprint of a deliberate oscillation, invisible in the raw time-domain trace but unmistakable once decomposed into its constituent frequencies. This is precisely the diagnostic power the Fourier transform provides: it reveals structure — hidden, regular periodicity — that isn't visible at all when a signal is only examined as a sequence of values over time.

But this diagnostic only works if the underlying high-resolution signal was recorded somewhere in the first place. If only the sensor's own periodic, averaged output was ever retained — the very quantity whose sampling window is the alleged vulnerability — there is no finer-grained record left to run a spectral analysis against. Whatever oscillation happened between samples, if it happened, existed briefly in the real world and then was gone, with nothing downstream ever having captured it at a resolution fine enough to prove it occurred. This is not a separate problem from sampling. It's the same problem, one step further along: a sampling rate doesn't just determine what a system sees in the moment. It determines, permanently, what evidence exists afterward for anyone trying to reconstruct what actually happened — which may be part of why this particular story never got a public, numbers-on-the-table resolution at all.

## Sampling is a budget, spent before anyone asks

The deeper lesson, independent of any one contested example, is that a sampling rate is never a neutral technical detail quietly handled by whoever configured the sensor. It's a decision, made in advance, about which slice of reality a system will be capable of perceiving at all — a decision that trades cost, storage, and processing load against the range of behavior the system can actually detect. Sample too slowly, and fast, meaningful events don't just get missed; they get actively misrepresented as something else, something slower and false, in exactly the way an aliased frequency masquerades as a real one. Sample as fast as physically possible, and the cost — in storage, bandwidth, and processing — can become enormous, often for frequencies of behavior that will never actually occur in practice.

This tension between capability and cost is a genuine budget, spent in advance, often by someone who never has to answer for what it left unable to see — a tension this series will return to directly once it reaches the question of observability. For now, the more immediate question this chapter leaves open is a different one: even once a signal has been sampled correctly, at a sufficient rate, a separate and equally old problem remains — how do you tell the difference between the part of what you've captured that's real, and the part that's just noise? That is the subject of the next chapter, through a story about a persistent hiss two radio astronomers spent months trying, and failing, to make go away.
